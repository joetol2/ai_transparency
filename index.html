What's new

Updated August 17, 2026. Legislative and regulatory developments since the August 3, 2026 snapshot.

State watchlist

California synthetic performer ad disclosure bill clears Appropriations

The California Assembly Appropriations Committee reported SB 1050 with a do pass as amended recommendation by a vote of 12 to 1 on August 13, 2026, and the bill was ordered to a second reading, according to the official bill history. SB 1050 would require clear and conspicuous disclosure when AI-generated synthetic performers appear in commercial advertisements, adding a California obligation alongside New York's in-force synthetic performer disclosure law. An Assembly floor vote is the next step before the measure could reach Governor Newsom. It is the most advanced California advertising disclosure bill of the session.

Q-S impact: No immediate compliance change. SB 1050 is proposed legislation, not current law. Monitor for Assembly floor vote.

California advances digital replica impersonation bill SB 1111

California SB 1111 received a unanimous do pass recommendation, 15 to 0, from the Assembly Appropriations Committee on August 13, 2026 and was ordered to a third reading, according to its bill history. The measure clarifies that the state's criminal false impersonation statute reaches the use of a digital replica to impersonate another person. A companion deepfake extortion measure, SB 1015, was held under submission at the same August 13 hearing and is stalled for the session, per its record.

Q-S impact: No immediate compliance change. SB 1111 is proposed legislation pending a Senate floor vote. Monitor for enactment before year end.

Two California AI bills stall in Assembly Appropriations

AB 412, which would require AI developers to document and disclose copyrighted training materials on request by rights holders, was held under submission in the Assembly Appropriations Committee on August 13, 2026. SB 1146, which would require disclosure when a health-related consumer ad uses a digital replica depicted as a health care provider, was held under submission the same day. Both are effectively stalled for the current session, narrowing the set of California proposals still moving toward a floor vote.

Q-S impact: No immediate compliance change from either bill. Both are stalled and unlikely to become law this session. No new review obligations from this development.

Executive action

White House reviews voluntary frontier AI model framework with developers

The White House convened major AI developers, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Meta, and Nvidia, on August 4, 2026 to review a finalized voluntary framework for federal review of advanced frontier models, according to CNBC and Fortune. The framework offers a 30-day pre-release window for government cybersecurity evaluation of covered closed-source models and may not be used to create a mandatory licensing or preclearance system. It carries out the June 2, 2026 executive order on advanced AI innovation and security, and open-weight models fall outside its scope.

Q-S impact: No immediate compliance change. This is a voluntary framework directed at model developers, not agencies or advertisers. Monitor for any binding regulatory follow-on.

Two AI transparency measures passed by the New York legislature remain unsigned as of mid-August 2026, with Governor Kathy Hochul holding a signing deadline of December 31, 2026, per a Transparency Coalition update. Compliance framework

The legal landscape supports one clear conclusion. This is not one blended AI-disclosure topic. Q-S should treat the landscape as separate compliance lanes, because the business owner, approval path, and required control change depending on the issue.

Lane 1

Commercial advertising and deception

This covers misleading synthetic claims, fake endorsements, fabricated demos, cloned spokesperson risk, and some call-based voice use.

Lane 2

Rights, consent, and talent

This covers digital replicas, voice cloning, performer contracts, deceased personalities, publicity rights, and model-related constraints.

Lane 3

Provider, platform, and provenance obligations

This covers tool-side duties, moderation workflows, provenance handling, platform removal processes, and vendor review.

Lane 4

Election and political synthetic media

This covers campaign communications, candidate misrepresentation, political disclosures, and issue-adjacent publisher risk.

What is live now

Q-S operates under a real and growing baseline of enacted law. The list below distinguishes laws that are currently operative from laws that have been enacted but whose obligations have not yet begun. A law that has been signed is not necessarily operative today.

In force now

FTC Act, unfair or deceptive acts and practices authority

Effective: In force

Treat this as the default federal backstop. AI use does not reduce the underlying truth-in-advertising burden.

United States

TAKE IT DOWN Act, platform removal duties

Effective: May 19, 2026 for FTC-enforced Section 3

FTC enforcement is active as of May 19, 2026. Warning letters have been sent to 16 major platforms and 12 nudify AI sites. Platform governance, moderation workflows, and crisis-response procedures should be reviewed against the 48-hour removal requirement now.

California AI Transparency Act (SB 942)

Operative date per official statute: January 1, 2026. The dates "August 2, 2026" and "January 1, 2027 (hosting-platform duties)" appeared in prior versions of this page and originated from secondary sources. Neither has been independently verified against the official California enforcement record or enrolled bill text. Legal review may be appropriate for clarifying current compliance obligations under this statute.

SB 942 is primarily a tool-governance and provenance issue, not a blanket creative-labeling law. Vendor review should confirm whether tools meet the statute's obligations for machine-readable provenance data and a public detection tool.

Operative date source: Official CA Legislature (Tier 1). "Aug. 2, 2026" and Jan. 1, 2027 hosting duties: secondary source only, not verified. Human review recommended.

Connecticut's AI omnibus law includes synthetic content provenance requirements for covered AI systems. Tool vendors and platform operators distributing synthetic media in Connecticut should review the phase-in timeline.

Hawaii
PRIMARY SOURCE

HB 2137, deepfake and synthetic performer advertising law

Signed: July 16, 2026. Prohibits knowingly publishing a realistic AI-generated imitation of a person without consent when used in advertising or to commit fraud, defamation, harassment, or other unlawful conduct. Requires conspicuous disclosure of a synthetic performer in commercial advertising when the advertiser has actual knowledge. Damages reach $25,000 per advertisement. Creative and trafficking review for Hawaii-facing ads should include a synthetic performer disclosure check.

Source: Hawaii Legislature, enrolled bill text (Tier 1).

Hawaii
SECONDARY SOURCE

SB 3001, conversational AI disclosure and minor safety law

Signed: July 16, 2026. Requires conversational AI services to disclose that a user is interacting with artificial intelligence, with repeated disclosures for services directed at minors. Prohibits AI from impersonating licensed mental health professionals or using features that increase emotional dependence in minors. Enforceable as an unfair or deceptive practice, with penalties up to $1,000 per violation and a $1 million cap per operator.

Source: Transparency Coalition (Tier 3). Primary source (HI legislature) not confirmed. Human review recommended before relying on specific penalty figures.

SECONDARY SOURCE

FAIR Act, ban on algorithmic rent-setting software

Signed: July 20, 2026. Prohibits landlords from using algorithmic pricing systems that rely on competitors' nonpublic rent and vacancy data to coordinate rental prices or occupancy levels. New Jersey is the fourth state to restrict rent-setting algorithms. Direct advertising relevance is limited, but the law adds to the enacted baseline of state constraints on AI-driven and algorithmic decision systems.

Source: NJBiz (Tier 2). Primary source (NJ Legislature) not confirmed.

Federal watchlist

These proposals are not yet law, but each carries material risk for Q-S operations and requires active monitoring.

Federal watch

NO FAKES Act of 2026

Status: S.4591, NO FAKES Act of 2026. The Senate Judiciary Committee approved the bill by voice vote on June 18, 2026. The bill was subsequently placed on the Senate legislative calendar, setting up a potential floor vote. No floor vote has been scheduled as of July 27, 2026, and no House companion has advanced.

Why it matters: Very high relevance. The bill would increase statutory penalties to $750,000 per work for platforms distributing unauthorized AI replicas and adds federal preemption of overlapping state replica rules. This could reshape celebrity simulation, synthetic spokespeople, AI voiceovers, and platform hosting obligations.

Monitor: Senate floor scheduling; First Amendment amendments sought by Senators Padilla, Cruz, Lee, and Schmitt; whether the bill is folded into the White House and Blackburn preemption package; platform safe harbor treatment

Federal watch

COPIED Act of 2025

Status: Active. Introduced and referred to the Senate Commerce Committee.

Why it matters: Very high relevance for publishers, media companies, agencies, and platforms because it pushes toward federal provenance, content-authentication, and watermark-adjacent infrastructure for synthetic or synthetically modified content.

Monitor: Commerce Committee action, any narrowing of the covered-tools definition, private-right-of-action treatment, and how FTC or state AG enforcement would be structured in final text

Federal watch

TRAIN Act, House version

Status: Senate companion S.2455 passed Senate Judiciary Committee 6-2 on June 8, 2026, and now awaits full Senate consideration. House version H.R.7209 remains in House Judiciary.

Why it matters: High relevance for agencies, publishers, studios, and media companies because it could increase discovery pressure on model vendors, change diligence expectations, and alter enterprise contracting around training-data provenance.

Monitor: Full Senate vote on S.2455; House Judiciary activity on H.R.7209; and whether the bill grows from a discovery mechanism into broader training-data disclosure requirements

Federal watch

Great American AI Act (discussion draft)

Status: Discussion draft released June 4, 2026 by Reps. Obernolte and Trahan. Not yet a numbered bill. More than 200 state legislators signed a June 16, 2026 letter opposing the three-year state preemption provision, and the White House and Senator Blackburn are negotiating a separate package that would carry similar preemption language.

Why it matters: High relevance to the entire compliance landscape. The draft proposes a three-year federal preemption of state AI development laws, which could suspend enforcement of several state synthetic media and digital replica statutes. Thirty-six state attorneys general have raised objections.

Monitor: Whether it becomes a numbered bill; which state laws would be preempted; how the preemption scope is defined in introduced text; whether it merges with the White House and Blackburn package; state AG and legislator response

Federal watch

FTC proposed policy statement on suppression of accuracy in AI

Status: Proposed policy statement issued July 1, 2026 and published in the Federal Register July 7, 2026. Public comment closed July 31, 2026. Final statement pending as of August 3, 2026.

Why it matters: High relevance for the Section 5 backbone that governs AI-assisted commercial claims. The statement asserts that steering an AI model away from a correct output without clear and conspicuous disclosure can be a deceptive act, and it argues that conflicting state AI laws are impliedly preempted, citing Colorado's AI Act as an example.

Monitor: Whether the statement is finalized, how broadly the deception theory reaches AI-generated advertising outputs, and which state AI laws the FTC treats as preempted

Commercial versus political classification

A consistent misreading of the legal landscape conflates election laws with commercial advertising rules. The classifications below draw a firm line between the two.

Important: The strongest current advertiser-facing state signal is New York's synthetic performer disclosure law. California SB 942 matters, but it applies primarily to provider-side and tool-governance obligations, not advertiser disclosure directly.
Direct commercial advertising relevance

FTC Act Section 5, unfair or deceptive acts or practices

Where teams could overread it: Teams sometimes treat Section 5 as if it creates an AI-specific label rule. It does not. The problem is deception, not AI use by itself.

Why teams should still care: Brand, agency, and publisher teams still need it because any synthetic content that changes the truthfulness of a commercial claim can trigger FTC exposure.

Direct commercial advertising relevance

FTC Rule on Impersonation of Government and Businesses, 16 C.F.R. Part 461

Where teams could overread it: Teams may overread it as a broad ban on all AI avatars or branded bots. It targets false impersonation, not every synthetic interface.

Why teams should still care: Platforms and publishers should still care because ads or messages that imply false affiliation with a company can create enforcement and inventory-review risk.

Direct commercial advertising relevance

FTC proposed amendment adding impersonation of individuals

Where teams could overread it: Teams could overread it as banning all fictional AI characters. The proposal is about impersonating an individual, not about clearly fictional or disclosed synthetic figures.

Why teams should still care: Even before finalization, publisher and brand review teams should care because it signals the FTC's likely next enforcement direction on human impersonation in ads and media.

Direct commercial advertising relevance

FCC Declaratory Ruling that AI-generated voices are covered by TCPA restrictions

Where teams could overread it: Teams may overread it as covering all synthetic audio in all media. It is about calls governed by the TCPA, not every video, ad, or podcast use of AI voice.

Why teams should still care: Brands, agencies, and vendors should still care because call campaigns are a real commercial channel, and synthetic voice does not escape consent requirements.

Direct commercial advertising relevance

New York GBL § 396-b, synthetic performer ad disclosure law

Where teams could overread it: Teams can overread it as applying to all AI-generated content or all manipulated media. It is narrower and turns on the defined synthetic performer concept plus statutory exemptions.

Why teams should still care: Publishers and distributors should care because ad acceptance, trafficking, and market-targeting review for New York-facing campaigns may need a disclosure checkpoint.

Direct commercial advertising relevance

California AB 2602, digital replica contract law for living performers

Where teams could overread it: Teams often overread it as a general ban on AI performers. It is really a contract-validity and consent-specificity rule for covered performer relationships.

Why teams should still care: Business affairs, production, and brand teams should care because an ad or branded-content campaign can fail at the contract layer long before it becomes a disclosure issue.

What do I need to do?

Scenario-based guidance for common Q-S situations. These are entry points, not approval decisions. Route the question to the right owner and flag early rather than late.

Scenario

AI-generated or synthetic person in advertising

If the work uses a computer-generated person who is entirely fictional and does not resemble a real individual, the primary concern is FTC deceptive-practices authority over false impressions. No current law requires disclosure that a fictitious human model is AI-generated, but claims made by or attributed to that figure still carry the standard truth-in-advertising burden.

If there is any resemblance to a real person, route to Business Affairs for a likeness and publicity rights review before production proceeds.

Route: Legal for claim review. Business Affairs if any real person is implicated.

Scenario

Cloning or synthesizing a real person's voice or likeness

California AB 2602 (living performers) and AB 1836 (deceased personalities) apply now if the individual has an entertainment industry connection. The NO FAKES Act would extend a similar right federally if enacted. New York requires performer consent and disclosure for synthetic performers in covered commercial advertising.

This scenario requires clearance before production regardless of how realistic the output appears. Do not proceed without confirmed consent documentation.

Route: Business Affairs for consent and rights clearance. Legal for jurisdiction-specific disclosure requirements.

Scenario

Generative AI in creative production

If AI tools are used to generate visual or audio content, check whether the tools used meet California SB 942 provenance requirements (operative date January 1, 2026, per official statute, though dates warrant verification). If the output enters a commercial product, the truth-in-advertising baseline applies to any factual claim in or associated with the output.

Training data sourcing for tools is a vendor diligence question, not a campaign-level disclosure obligation under current law, though AB 412 (currently stalled) would change this if enacted.

Route: Procurement and AI governance for tool review. Legal if output contains product claims.

Scenario

Campaign runs across multiple states

State obligations are not uniform. California, New York, and Texas are active jurisdictions with distinct requirements. A campaign that runs in all three may face different disclosure, consent, and platform-notification obligations depending on the content and medium.

Multi-state campaigns with synthetic performers or AI-generated voices should be reviewed for each target jurisdiction before trafficking, not after the buy is placed.

Route: Legal for jurisdiction mapping. Business Affairs for consent documentation by state.

Scenario

Political or election-related creative

Political advertising is a separate compliance lane. Several states require disclosure that AI was used in political advertising, and some restrict synthetic depictions of candidates. These obligations apply regardless of whether the content meets the federal definition of "political advertising."

Treat election-adjacent content as political for review purposes if there is any doubt. The cost of a late escalation in a political context is substantially higher than the cost of an unnecessary review.

Route: Legal for political-content review before production. Escalate immediately if a real candidate's voice or likeness is used in any form.
